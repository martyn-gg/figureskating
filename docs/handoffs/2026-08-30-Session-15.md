## `bootDir` has three rules, and the third is the reach

The specification said a picked boot is "planted, pitched past `MAX_BLADE_PITCH`", which
reads as the skating branch with a bigger number. **It is not, and the formula being already
written above it is exactly what makes the mistake tempting.**

A blade takes its direction from the tracing because a blade on the ice can only lie along
its own line. **A pick is not travelling** — it is a jab; the teeth go in and stay in one
spot while the skater goes past — so it has no line, and what points the toe is the REACH:
the horizontal direction from the hip to the foot. Taking the tracing instead points the toe
*at the skater* on the only move that needs it, because a toe loop reaches back on a backward
edge and back there is +t while the tracing runs −t.

**`bootDir` also stopped taking `skating` as an argument.** Seven callers each computed
`onIceOf(pose, which) === 'blade'` and handed the answer in: one derivation in seven places,
and a boolean free to disagree with the pose it came from, which is the shape `rig-math.js`'s
own opening comment warns about. Not in the specification; it fell out of touching every call
site at once, which is what a change of this shape is for.

## The renderer's `skating` was three decisions under one name

Weight, edge colour and the edge dot moved together for as long as being on the ice meant
being on a blade. A picked foot is bearing load — it must not draw pale, in free weight, or
vanish with the free-foot toggle — and it has no biting edge, so it must take neither a
colour nor a dot. **`planted` decides the first, `bladed` the other two**, and the four glyph
functions take the contact rather than a boolean. A picked runner draws in ink with its teeth
at full weight, which is the picture: the runner up out of the ice and the teeth in it.

## `shin.mjs` was extended to picks and put back

Worth recording because the argument for extending it was good and wrong. A free boot is
built square to the shin by construction, so its shin-to-boot angle is an identity — that is
why the file iterates blades only. A picked boot's direction is authored, so the angle looked
falsifiable.

It is not. The lean `shin.mjs` measures is the shin against a boot up-axis built from **world
up**, which is what "lean inside the boot" means while the boot is near flat and stops meaning
anything at 80°. Use the boot's real up-axis instead and it comes from the knee, one iteration
from the shin being measured, and the angle collapses towards an identity again. Written up in
the file so it is not tried a third time.

## The ankle decides the pose, and it is ANKLE_MAX read backwards

A free foot authors its ankle angle and the boot's direction follows. **A picked foot authors
the boot's direction — pitch, on the ice — and the ankle angle follows**, as whatever the shin
and that direction leave between them. Same boot, same allowance, so the same limit, and this
is **the first time that constant has bitten on a pose rather than on a number somebody typed**.
`freefoot.mjs` asserts it; `npm run ankle` prints the evidence as a third table.

**Three bands, not a slope:**

| hip height | steepest legal boot |
|---|---|
| 62 cm and below | **89°** — standing on end, which is what a jab is |
| 64 to 76 cm | **no legal pick at all**, at any pitch or any reach |
| 78 cm and above | **4 to 12°** — barely past the 3.5° a blade already has; a scuff |

The only way to tilt the boot further with the toe on the ice is to tilt the whole leg, and
the only way to do that is to sink. **Which is what a skater does before they pick.** Nobody
authored that; the constraint did. It is also why `toePick` sits at a hip of 54 and reads as a
deep sit — that look is `ANKLE_MAX` at 30° talking, and 30 is still read off a study of injury.

Sinking is not free either: `shin.mjs` will not have a deep knee with the blade under the hip,
so the skating foot travels out from under it as the hip drops. Same fact that shapes the
teapot and the sit spin, arriving for the third time.

## A pick is the first contact fixed to the ice

Every foot in `moves.js` is authored relative to the hip, and only the reference blade is
pinned to the path. A gliding blade travels with the skater, so this has never cost anything.
**A pick stays where it was put.** Held through a real span of travel its hip-relative position
would have to sweep backwards by however far the hip went — 168 cm over `toePick`'s arc, out of
reach within the first centimetres.

So `toePick` is a **held position and not a movement**, `twoFoot` fashion, and the arc beneath
it is where the skating blade is going rather than a claim that the toe is sliding with it.
Expressing a contact that stays put needs an anchor the rig has not got, and that is a bigger
change than this one was.

**The entry is not drawn, and it was tried first.** As a movement — glide, sink, reach, pick —
it failed twice, both times for real reasons. The free boot descending onto the pick reaches
**78° from level over 92 frames**, because a boot square to a shin that steep points at the
ice. The top-down glyph turns **155° in the single frame** where `bootDir` changes rule,
against the 95 `continuity.mjs` allows across a landing. Neither is a defect in the pick: they
say the frames between *free* and *picked* are a state the model has not got — the foot is
neither hanging nor planted — and inventing one to make a probe animate would be authoring pose
data to satisfy a checker.

## The plan glyph never pivoted about its contact, and no checker could see it

**All seventeen checkers were green and the rear view drew half the boot underneath the ice.**

`bootSide` has always shifted itself so that whatever part of the blade is touching sits at the
origin, because the origin is placed on the ice. The plan branch never did — and never needed
to, because **no boot had ever been both plan-drawn and touching**: a skating boot cannot reach
the plan glyph (its up-axis points up, never at a camera beside or behind the skater), and a
free boot has no contact to pivot about. `along` was zero every time that code ran, for six
sessions.

The general form, now in `rig-verify`: when a new case reaches an old branch, the question is
not whether the branch is correct — it is **which of its inputs have only ever had one value**.
Those are unexercised by definition, and no checker built on the old cases will say so.

Fixed inside the matrix's own translation rather than a wrapping group, writing a bare `0`
where there is nothing to shift, so **every frame that existed before today draws
byte-identically**. Verified as one hash over **189 standalone frames of nine moves** before
the change and after it — which is worth doing on any render change claiming to be inert, and
which caught a `0` that had become `0.0000`.

## Broken on purpose

| mutation | reported |
|---|---|
| every pick set to a blade's pitch | 3 of 3 |
| every reference blade set to a pick's | 37 of 40 |
| the picked pose's hip raised 20 cm | 3 feet |
| an ankle angle written onto a picked foot | 3 feet |

The second row is the one a keyframe flag could never have produced. **An exemption can only
excuse a pose, never hold it to anything** — replace it with a declaration and the same file
gets a second assertion pointing the other way for free: a blade must be inside ±3.5°, and a
pick must be *outside* it, or the pose is claiming a contact its geometry does not make.
